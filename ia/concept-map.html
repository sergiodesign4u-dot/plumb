Thirteen screens, and every one of them answerable to a job

The base layer: what objects the product is made of, which screens exist and why, how a person moves between them, and a matrix that proves the coverage rather than asserting it. Seven screens are MVP, and three of those carry the whole risk.

01

Entities

Objects first, screens second. Screens only show objects, and objects grow out of jobs. This inventory is the foundation of everything below it.

A fork the flows had to settle

The reading was left open here: either a stored object, or a moment encoded in the link. Flow 3 showed there is no third option. If the link does not carry the moment it was read, the state degrades to "the definition changed at some point", which asks the reader to remember when they took the number, and that is the very thing they came to us to avoid. It travels in the link.

Under question, because no job sits under them. The workspace lives in the funnel metrics and is needed for tenancy and billing, and it closes no job. The link as an object is either a property of the metric or an analytics event, not something a person handles.

Deliberately not entities. A state is not an entity, and this is the most important line in the inventory. "As of", "source is down" and "definition changed after this was saved" are computed and store nothing. The moment a state gets its own entity it gets a list, a status and a queue, and we have built an incident tool, which the design principles forbid outright. The registry is not an entity, it is a view over metrics. And the reader has no user object at all: the primary persona has no account by decision, so nothing in the product represents them. That is why there is no "my profile", no "my saved" and no "my settings" for the person the interface is built around.

Search gets no screen of its own. It is a component on the registry and in the analyst's global navigation, not a place, so no screen was invented to fill a row in the matrix.

There is no "what changed in the definition" screen, and that is not an omission. The To-Be considered a diff of two definitions and rejected it, because it answers a question the reader did not ask. The previous definition lives as a line inside the changed state on the card.

The one thing that can silently cancel our chosen UX pattern

The pattern is evidence on the claim: trust information lives on the object being read and travels with it. The map implements it. The registry is the exception to watch, because a list with search is the rejected "catalog and search" pattern in pure form. It stays as the analyst's instrument, and the guard is a single rule: it must never appear in the reader's navigation. The moment it becomes the reader's entry point, we have changed the pattern without saying so.

03

Navigation model

The finding that shapes this whole section

The primary persona has no global navigation, and that is the design rather than a gap. The reader arrives by a link, holds no account, and the strongest measurement in this research says a destination they are expected to travel to is entered once or twice a year. Global navigation in this product belongs to the secondary persona. Written down because it is the kind of thing a later stage silently repairs by adding a header and a menu, which would turn the card into a page of a site the reader has no reason to be on.

Global navigation, which is the analyst's
ItemCluster it entersThe job behind it
MetricsThe registryAnswer once; the definition stops living in a head
SourcesConnect a sourceThe main job as a precondition: no source, no age, no state
Account cornerSign in, workspace, planNone. Tenancy and billing, marked jobless rather than dressed up

Two items, not the three to five a guideline would suggest, and the range was not padded. There are two analyst intents in this product. A third item would have to be invented from other people's menus, which is exactly what this stage forbids. Search is global but is not an item: it is a persistent affordance over the registry.

Arrival is the answer. Everything is within three taps and the primary path is at zero, which is the entire point of the product.

First run and returning run land in different places, and the critique found that neither artefact said so. On the first run for a workspace, sign-in leads to connecting a source, because a registry with no source behind it can hold nothing. On every run after that, sign-in lands on the registry. The map drew one order and the flow drew the other, and both were right about a case neither named.

Global

For the analyst: metrics, sources, search, the account corner. For the reader: nothing. The card carries its own affordances and nothing else.

Contextual

The source layer and the send action from the card, defining a metric from the registry or from a source, sign-in only when nobody is signed in.

Deep

Workspace and people, plan and seats, and the public surface, which is not in-app navigation at all.

What the flattening cost, named rather than defended

The reader can only reach a metric somebody sent them a link to. No browsing, no second metric, no way back to a first one. Giving them the registry would fix it and would cancel the chosen pattern, so the cost is accepted deliberately. Its consequence is the single red node in the flows: a reader who lands on a card they may not see has nowhere to go.

This is the gate on the size of the product: the detail layer writes up the nodes, the wireframes draw the screens and the visual stage colours them, and all three start from the MVP subset rather than the whole map.

A narrower subset still, and this is the build order

The first test of the riskiest assumption needs four capabilities, and they touch three screens: the card, its source layer, and sending it. The analyst's three MVP screens are what make those work with real data, not what the test needs.

Defects, job orphans first because they cost more

Job orphans: none. Every functional job has at least one screen where it is closed. That matters more than the screen side: a job with no screen means a person has nowhere to do their work, while a screen with no job only means we built ballast.

Screen orphans: two, and both resolved rather than left hanging
OrphanDecision
D2. Workspace and peopleDeliberately in the backlog, and the row stays empty. A workspace with more than one analyst has to be administrable, so it is not deleted; it serves tenancy rather than anybody's job, so it earns no MVP surface. An empty marked row is an honest state, and a tick added to tidy the table would be the defect this stage exists to catch
D3. Plan and seatsDeliberately in the backlog. It hangs off the business model hypothesis, which is still open: this market has no reader-pricing norm to inherit, and we have not decided ours
One MVP screen carries no X at all, and it is named rather than quietly ticked

Sign in. It is not an orphan, because it is a precondition of three jobs. It is also the screen most likely to grow: sign-in is a gate, not a place, and if it ever acquires product surface, this is the line to come back to.

Emotional and social jobs, and what actually supports them

These are not closed by a screen. Asking where a person does them is meaningless, and a tick invented to fill the row is worse than an honest gap. The column that matters is the mechanism: a state, a piece of microcopy, a proof of trust, a behaviour after an error, an order of blocks. The detail layer takes states and trust proofs from this table, and the Voice stage takes its input to tone from it.

A risk in that last row, named here rather than discovered at the visual stage

The mechanism that builds an analyst's reputation is the same one that exposes it: their name sits on every number, including the ones that break. Nothing in the research says analysts want that. If they do not, the attribution that serves the reader is the thing that makes the analyst resist the product.
